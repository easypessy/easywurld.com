> **The surprise has to be legal for the character.**

A child eating cardboard is absurd — **but you accept it long enough to keep watching**, because a
child is exactly the kind of person who would. An adult would not be believed for a second, and the
video would feel like a trick. Same joke, wrong casting, dead video.

---

## 2. The mechanic, named

There are two things working here, and they are different:

### A. The interrupt (why you stop)

Your brain runs on prediction. Scrolling is a semi-automatic state: it predicts "another talking
head, another product shot" and stops looking. Something that breaks the prediction forces the
brain to stop and classify it. Psychologists call the underlying reflex the **orienting response** —
an involuntary turn of attention toward anything novel or unexpected. You cannot opt out of it,
any more than you can opt out of flinching. *(High confidence: this reflex is well established.
The marketing version — "pattern interrupt" — comes from sales training, where it meant breaking a
prospect's conversational autopilot.)*

**Your version:** a bitten cardboard in a child's hands. Prediction broken in one frame.

### B. The held question (why you stay)

An interrupt buys you about two seconds. It does not buy you thirty. What held you for twenty more
was a question the video refused to answer: *is she actually eating cardboards?*

That is **information-gap theory** — curiosity fires when you become aware of a *specific* gap
between what you know and what you want to know, and it feels like an itch, not a mood.
*(High confidence: George Loewenstein, Carnegie Mellon, 1994, in* Psychological Bulletin*. One of
the most cited ideas in consumer psychology.)*

Loewenstein's three conditions are the useful part, because they tell you exactly why some
interrupts work and most do not:

| Condition | In the cardboard video | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| You must know enough to sense something is missing | You know children eat snacks, not cardboard | The viewer must already understand the normal version |
| The gap must be **specific**, not vague | "Is she eating that?" — a yes/no | "Something amazing happens" is not a gap. It is noise. |
| The gap must feel **bridgeable** | 20 more seconds will answer it | The answer must be within the video, or it is a cheat |

### C. The third thing — the one your instinct added

**The character has to make the twist believable.** That is not in either textbook. It is the
difference between *surprising* and *random*.

---

## 3. The three conditions of a working interrupt

Write these on a wall. Every interrupt you make must pass all three.

| # | Condition | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **1. A pattern must exist first** | You need 2–3 seconds of "normal" before you break it. The reveal is only funny because the first three seconds were ordinary. | You open with chaos. Chaos is not a surprise, it is a blur. |
| **2. The break must be plausible for the character** | A child, a market woman, a tired delivery rider, a shop owner at 11pm — each one is *allowed* certain behaviour and not others. | You use a "weird" visual with no reason it belongs to who is on screen. Then it reads as a stunt. |
| **3. The break must be paid off** | The reveal answers the exact question the setup created, and it lands **at 60–85% of the runtime**, not at the end. | You never reveal. That is a tease, and a tease loses the viewer permanently — including on your next video. |

**The failure mode to fear most:** *unmotivated surprise.* Random imagery gets the first two
seconds and then the thumb leaves anyway. It also trains people not to trust your first frame —
and 2026 audiences punish that. (Reported by practitioners: platforms now track **early exit**
— leaving within three seconds after a hook — as a negative signal. **Low–Medium confidence**, but
the direction is consistent across sources.)

---

## 4. The timing (this is where most people get it wrong)

The cardboard video's build was long. Yours should not be. Here is the shape:

```
0–2s    THE ORDINARY        a normal thing, shot normally
        (pattern formed)
2–4s    THE BREAK           the one detail that does not fit
        (pattern interrupted)
4–15s   THE HELD QUESTION   the normal thing continues, quietly, while the
                            question sits in the viewer's chest
15–25s  THE REVEAL          the answer — must answer the exact question
25–30s  THE TURN BACK       one line that makes it mean something
```

Two rules:

- **The break must be visible in frame one or two.** Not spoken. Seen. Silent-visual interrupts
  outperform text-overlay hooks by a wide margin in every vendor benchmark I found — treat the
  number as **Low confidence**, but note that the *direction* is unanimous across sources.
- **The reveal is at 60–85% of the runtime, never the last second.** You need 3–5 seconds after the
  reveal for the meaning to land. A reveal at 0:29 of a 0:30 video means the viewer leaves with the
  answer and no reason to feel anything.

## 5. Eight business versions (no props, no budget, no lies)

You do not need a child and a cardboard. You need the same three conditions. These all work on a
phone, on a desk, in one take.

| # | The ordinary | The break | The reveal | Why the character makes it plausible |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | You open a Lagos shop's Instagram | Every post is the same photo of the same product | You read the comments: 40 people asked "how much?" and none got an answer | A real shop, a real page, unedited |
| 2 | You type a normal customer message | You send it to five real businesses | Who replied in under an hour, and who never did | You are genuinely a customer |
| 3 | You hold two phones | One shows a page on data, one on WiFi | The page takes 9 seconds to load on data, 1 on WiFi | Cheap Android, real network |
| 4 | You print a stranger's Instagram page | You read it out loud as a stranger would | The parts that make no sense spoken aloud | Paper is absurd, which is why it holds |
| 5 | You count out loud while scrolling | You scroll a page for exactly four seconds | You can name what they sell — or you cannot | Four seconds is a real test |
| 6 | You show a "beautiful" page | You cover the logo | Nobody can tell which business it is | All pages look the same, and that is the point |
| 7 | You screen-record your own WhatsApp | The chat where you asked a price 3 days ago | Still no reply | This one is your own phone, so it is true |
| 8 | You hold a ₦5,000 note next to a screenshot | You ask what it buys | One pack, or one hour of a designer | The note is real, the comparison is honest |

**Notice what is missing:** no dancing, no shouting, no fake emergencies, no "wait for it".
Every one of these is a **real observation** with the shape of a surprise. That is the version you
can defend in the comments.

## 6. The honesty limits (this is the part that keeps the account alive)

| Do | Never |
|---|---|
| Use a real page, shown openly, without insult | Blur a real business's name and mock it — that is punching down with extra steps |
| Show your own phone, your own inbox | Stage a "customer" conversation and present it as real. If it is a joke, it is a meme — labelled as a format, never as evidence |
| Reveal the answer inside the video | Promise a reveal and withhold it — that is a tease, and it costs you the next video too |
| Say "this is the fourth page I checked today" — if it is | Invent a number of businesses you checked |

**One specific trap:** do not fake the outcome. "I fixed this page and they sold out" is an
invented testimonial if it did not happen. If you *did* fix one, say exactly what happened, in the
smallest words available: "She added the price. Two people ordered that week. That is all I know."

## 8. What to measure

One number: **how many people stayed past 3 seconds.** That is the only thing an interrupt is for.

- If a video dies before 3 seconds → the **interrupt** failed. Change the first frame, not the message.
- If it survives 3 seconds and dies at 8 → the interrupt worked and the **promise** failed. The gap was vague, or the reveal was late.
- If it survives to the end and nobody shares it → the interrupt was fine and there was **no reason to pass it on**. That is a storytelling problem, not a hook problem.

*(TikTok shows a retention graph in the app. You do not need a tool for this. Look for the cliff,
and read the timestamp on it.)*
