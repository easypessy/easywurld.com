# 02 — The common villain, and the hero who fights it

*Your second entry. Including the part you said was still foggy — and it is the right part to be
foggy about.*

---

## 1. What the woman actually said, and what it is called

Her advice: **create a common enemy, and a hero to fight it. Get people on the side of the hero or
the villain — but we stay in the middle.**

She is describing something old and well documented. The most useful version of it is Donald
Miller's *Building a StoryBrand*, and the one line that matters is:

> **The customer is the hero. Your brand is the guide.**
> *(Medium–High confidence: the book is widely taught and its claims are consistent across
> practitioner sources; it is a framework, not a measured result.)*

Miller's reason is blunt, and it is the same thing every storytelling teacher says: **a brand that
casts itself as the hero has no seat left for the audience.** If you are Luke Skywalker, the viewer
is watching you. If they are Luke Skywalker and you are Obi-Wan, the viewer is *in* it.

And the villain — Miller's definition, which is the part most people get wrong — is the **root
cause** of the customer's problem. Not a competitor. Not a person. It should be:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| **Real** | Does it actually happen, this week, to people I can name? |
| **Singular** | Is it *one* thing? Two villains is no villain. |
| **Root** | If it were removed, would the problem go away — or just move? |
| **Relatable** | Would the customer nod before I finish the sentence? |
| **Not the customer** | Is the audience on the hero's side? |

## 2. The foggy part, answered

You said:

> "It should be in a way that if we don't join hands, we cannot actually solve the problem. But I
> don't want it to feel like you're now depending on them. Like they need us to help them."

You are circling a real trap, and it has a clean exit. The trap is this:

> **If you say "let's fight this together", you have made yourself a participant in their problem.**
> Participants need things. Guides do not.

So the fix is not in the wording. It is in the **casting**. Three roles, and you only ever hold one:

| Role | Who | What they want | Who asks for something |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The villain** | The system, the habit, the default — never a person and never the customer | To keep things as they are | **The villain** is the one taking. It takes customers, time, money. |
| **The hero** | The business owner reading the post | To stop losing what they already earned | Nobody. They already want it. |
| **The guide** | Us | To be the thing that is *useful at the moment of the fight* | Nobody. We name the enemy and hand over a tool. |

**Now read your own worry against that table.** Who is asking whom for help? The villain takes.
The hero already wants to win. The guide hands over a tool. **Nobody is begging.** The sentence
"we cannot solve this alone" stops being a plea and becomes a *fact about the enemy* — like saying
"you cannot lift a car alone." That is not the car asking for help.

### The division of labour (this is the sentence you were looking for)

Instead of *"we need each other"*, say what each side can actually do:

> **"I can see it because I'm outside. You can fix it because you're inside."**

That is the whole answer. It is a **division of labour, not a dependency**, because both jobs are
non-transferable:

- **Only you can be objective about their page.** You have no history with it. They have looked at
  it every day for two years and cannot see it any more.
- **Only they can answer their DMs.** You cannot reply to their customers. You can only give them
  the words.

Neither of those is neediness. Both are true. And notice that the second one keeps you honest:
you sell a script, not a rescue.

### Why you stay in the middle (your instinct was right)

You said "at all of it, we are going to be at the middle." That is not the weak position — it is
the position with the whistle. **You are the referee, not a player.**

A referee does not want either side to win. A referee says what happened, in public, so everyone
stops arguing about it. That is *literally* your position: *"I read your page and tell you the
truth."* The middle is the only place that sentence can be said from.

## 3. Cast the villain — carefully

You do not get to have a villain that is the customer. Look what happens when vendors do that:

> "Some online customers are just wicked." — the title of a real r/Nigeria thread.

That is a villain that makes the audience the enemy. It gets agreement from other sellers and it
repels everyone else, and it never sells anything, because a hero cannot fight himself.

**The right villain is the thing between them and the sale.** For this account there are four
candidates. Score them honestly:

| Candidate | Real? | Singular? | Root? | Relatable? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The silence after "how much?"** — the customer asks, the reply never comes, or the number comes with nothing attached | Yes, verifiable on real pages | Yes, one event | Yes — it is where the money leaves | Yes, every vendor has lived it | **Choose this one** |
| "The gurus" | Yes | No — a crowd, and you would be biting the hand that found you | No | Mixed | Reject |
| "Instagram's algorithm" | Yes | Yes | No — it is the weather, not the leak | Yes, but it makes people helpless | Reject as *lead* villain; usable as a side note |
| "Big brands with budgets" | Yes | Yes | No | Yes | Reject — it makes the audience small |

### The villain card

Print this. Every post that has a villain uses this one.

> **THE VILLAIN: the silence after "how much?"**
>
> A customer asks a price. What comes back is either nothing, or a number with no reason and no
> date. The customer says *"the interest will just off me"* — their words, not ours — and leaves.
> The vendor never learns they left.

**Its three levels** (Miller's framing, and it is the useful part — each level is a content lane):

| Level | What it is | What posts live here |
|---|---|---|
| **The problem** (external) | The mechanics: no price on the post, a reply three days late, no follow-up | Teardowns, checklists, "the four-second test" — the whole Teach tier |
| **The pain** (internal) | What it makes them feel: like a small shop, like they are begging for attention, embarrassed to chase | TRUE STORY, the memes, the chat screenshots — the Entertain tier |
| **The principle** (philosophical) | *A customer should not have to beg to know a price.* That is a belief, and it is defensible | The honest ads, the "we said no" posts, the manifesto |

**A month with only the external level is a tutorial channel.** A month with only the principle is
preaching. You need all three, which is why your existing 2:1:1 entertain/teach/sell mix already
works — it just needed a name.

## 4. What it sounds like

Ten lines, ready to use. All are honest, all name the villain, none of them beg.

1. "The customer asked the price four days ago. Look at the chat."
2. "Nobody is ignoring you on purpose. They are ignoring you because you never gave a number."
3. "This is not a bad page. This is a page with no price on it."
4. "'The interest will just off me.' A customer wrote that about a Lagos shop."
5. "Your customer is not wicked. Your customer is unanswered."
6. "Two vendors. Same product. One shows the price. Guess who sold out."
7. "The silence after 'how much?' has a cost. It is ₦5,000 at a time."
8. "I am not going to tell you your page is ugly. I am going to tell you where it leaks."
9. "You do not need more followers. You need to answer the ones you have."
10. "I can see it because I am outside. You can fix it because you are inside."

---

## 5. The five-beat structure for a villain post

```
1. NAME IT          the villain, in one sentence a customer would say
2. SHOW IT          a real page, a real chat, a real number — evidence, not opinion
3. THE COST         what it took: one customer, one week, one order
4. THE DIVISION     what only they can do, what only we can do
5. THE TOOL         the script, the line, the pack — small, specific, cheap
```

**Then stop.** No rallying cry, no "let's change Nigerian business together." That is the moment
guides turn into heroes, and the audience stops being in the story.

---

## 6. What would make this go wrong

| Failure | What it looks like | Why it kills the post |
|---|---|---|
| **The customer as villain** | "Customers are wicked", "Nigerians like to price-check and vanish" | Your audience is the customer's mirror. You just insulted the hero. |
| **A person as villain** | Naming a business, a guru, a competitor | Punching down, legally risky, and it makes you the hero of your own story |
| **Doom** | "Small businesses in Nigeria are dying" | A villain with no tool is despair. Despair does not get shared; it gets scrolled. |
| **Two villains** | Price *and* algorithm *and* gurus, in one post | A crowd is not an enemy. Pick one per post. |
| **The saviour** | "I will save your business" | That is the hero role. Hand it back. |

---

## 7. Test it in one week

Two numbers, and they are different from the ones you use for hooks:

- **Agreement comments.** When you name the villain, people write "this is me", "I'm guilty",
  "my sister's shop does this". Count them. Ten agreement comments on one post is a villain that
  lands. Zero means it is too abstract or too rude.
- **Saves on the tool posts.** A villain that is named well makes the *fix* worth keeping.

If a villain post gets agreement but no saves, you named the enemy well and handed over nothing.
That is a lecture. Go back to beat 5.

---

## 8. What this changes in the build

Nothing in the 330 posts needs rewriting. It changes **how you choose which one to post on a slow
day**: pick the post that names the silence, not the post that lists five tips.

And it gives December a spine that November does not have. November says *what* the four gaps are.
December can be built as a campaign **against one of them** — the silence — with every post,
every meme and every teardown on the same side.
