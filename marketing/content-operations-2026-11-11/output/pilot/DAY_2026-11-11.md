### CO1: The real work behind an enquiry
A reply is not the same thing as a usable enquiry.
Consider a fictional home-cleaning business. Its page gets messages, but customers still ask what a standard visit includes. The team cannot quote without answering the same scope question repeatedly. A practical test is to list the rooms covered, exclusions and the details needed for a quote. Track complete requests, not just message count. This is a decision-path example—not a claim about a client or a measured lift.
CTA: What information would your buyer need before requesting a quote?

### CO2: The first-screen exercise
A website first screen can be tested with three blanks.
Ask someone unfamiliar with the business to finish these sentences: This business helps ___. I can get ___. The next step is ___. If the answers are guesses, improve the message before polishing another animation. This is an editorial exercise, not a universal conversion benchmark.
CTA: Try it with someone outside your team.

### CO3: Volume versus clarity
More publishing cannot substitute for a clear buying path.
More posts may produce more chances to be seen. That does not tell a reader what is included, how pricing works or how to make a relevant request. Before adding another slot, define the question a qualified buyer should be able to answer after reading the post. Then compare the questions people actually ask. Treat this as a hypothesis to test, not a promised outcome.
CTA: What buyer question is missing from your content?

### CO4: Human-reviewed automation
The useful automation may be the one that knows when to stop.
A bounded enquiry workflow can read a verified coverage list, draft a response and route unknown areas to a person. The owner checks the draft before sending. This is an architecture example, not a live Easywurld bot. Exceptions, consent and data handling matter more than a demo that answers everything.

### CO5: A gate before creative
Who owns a claim before the design team types it?
