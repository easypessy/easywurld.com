# 02 — Consumer psychology & ethical persuasion

**Owner:** Audience insight / behavioral design · **Prerequisites:** 00–01 · **Pass evidence:** Audience decision map and ethical influence review

## Purpose and boundary

Use behavioral science to make choices understandable, relevant and easy to evaluate—not to diagnose individuals or manipulate them. Consumer behavior is contextual and heterogeneous. A principle from a laboratory, a different culture or a different product may not generalize to this audience or campaign.

## What to learn

- **Attention and perception:** limited attention, salience, contrast, fluency and cognitive load can affect what people notice and understand. They do not guarantee a click or purchase.
- **Motivation and behavior:** ability, opportunity and motivation interact; a reminder cannot solve an unavailable product, unclear offer or broken payment path. Diagnose friction before adding persuasive pressure.
- **Learning and memory:** distinctive, consistent cues and repeated exposure may support recognition. Repetition can also fatigue or annoy; brand assets and message consistency should serve recognition, not repetition for its own sake.
- **Choice architecture:** order, defaults, labels, comparison and effort influence decisions. Make material terms, costs, limitations and exit routes clear. Do not hide choices or make cancellation harder.
- **Social influence:** people use others' behavior and trusted recommendations as cues. Use only real, permissioned, accurately contextualized testimonials, reviews and endorsements; disclose relevant relationships. Never fabricate consensus.
- **Risk, trust and uncertainty:** perceived risk varies with the stakes and the person's context. Clear process, proof and honest qualification can help; unsupported certainty damages trust.
- **Emotion and identity:** stories can make an abstract issue concrete. Do not assume emotion is the only driver, exploit grief/fear/shame, or target vulnerabilities.
- **Habit and retention:** a useful service and an appropriate reminder matter more than an attention trap. Respect user control, frequency and consent.

## Audience decision map

For one audience situation, document: trigger/context → desired progress → current alternative → questions/risks → information needed → effort/barrier → available support → decision/action → post-action experience. For each statement, mark **observed**, **reported**, **inferred** or **hypothesized**, attach the evidence and confidence, and note whose perspective is missing.

## Ethical persuasion checklist

1. Is the offer and intended audience a real fit?
2. Is the promise accurate, specific, substantiated and qualified where necessary?
3. Are price, conditions, eligibility, material limitations and sponsorship clear at the decision point?
4. Is urgency/scarcity real, current and verifiable—or removed?
5. Can a person say no, compare, pause, unsubscribe or leave without obstruction?
6. Are we avoiding exploitation of children, crisis, health/financial distress or other vulnerability?
7. Does personalization use only appropriate, authorized data and avoid sensitive inference?
8. Does the message still feel fair if the audience sees the research method and our incentive?

## Connections

Strategy identifies the segment and decision context; psychology helps generate hypotheses about barriers; copy translates the hypothesis into plain language; design reduces comprehension friction; distribution controls exposure and frequency; analytics tests observable outcomes; governance checks fairness, privacy and claims.

## Assessment

Review three draft ads: one with a legitimate proof point, one using fake urgency, and one with a complicated CTA. Annotate the mechanism being attempted, evidence strength, audience risks and a less coercive revision. Then create one testable hypothesis with a counter-hypothesis. **Pass:** mechanism is framed as a possibility, not certainty; no vulnerability exploitation or dark pattern; material terms remain visible; test measures actual user action, not merely a proxy. **Fail/remediate:** remove coercive mechanics and repeat review.

## Common traps

- Treating “loss aversion,” “FOMO,” “social proof” or “neuroscience” as magic copy formulas.
- Calling a demographic bucket a psychologically meaningful audience.
- Inferring causation from a correlation or one campaign result.
- Treating a click as informed consent, satisfaction or business value.
- Optimizing conversion while ignoring refunds, complaints, user harm or poor-fit customers.
