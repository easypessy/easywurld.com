# How the best in the field actually build a post

An analysis of the structures behind the people who consistently go viral in
this niche — business, design and self-employed marketing — and what to take
from each. This is structural analysis of publicly visible work, not insider
data: I am describing **how the posts are built**, not claiming their numbers.

Read this once, then forget the names and keep the mechanics.

---

## The pattern underneath all of them

Strip away the personality and four things repeat:

1. **A hook that creates an unfinished thought.** The brain closes loops; it
   does not close opinions.
2. **One idea, defended with something specific** — a number, a quote, a
   receipt, a screenshot.
3. **A cost.** Someone lost money, time, a client, or face. Cost is what makes
   a reader feel rather than nod.
4. **A reason to pass it on.** Not "like and share" — a *person* the reader
   thought of while reading.

Everything below is a variation on those four.

## Alex Hormozi — arithmetic over adjectives

**What he does:** states a claim, then does visible maths on it. No design, no
metaphor, no "journey". Sentences short enough to read while walking.

**Why it works:** numbers feel like evidence, and the plainness signals
confidence. He also gives away the mechanism — *how* — not just the result.

**Steal:** the cost calculation on your own service. "A lost order is ₦45,000.
Two a week is ₦360,000 a month. The site costs less than one lost month."

**Don't steal:** the bluster. It reads as arrogance from anyone without his
track record.

**Break it:** sometimes the maths is cold. Day 9 of this pack uses a number to
open a feeling — "I almost quit in month three" — then does the maths.

## Dickie Bush & Nicolas Cole — Hook, Retain, Reward

**What they teach:** three jobs in sequence.

- **Hook** — earn the first line.
- **Retain** — earn the second line.
- **Reward** — pay the reader for staying.

They also insist on writing to **one specific person**, and on specificity as
the difference between writing and typing.

**Steal:** the three-job audit on every carousel. If slide 4 doesn't retain, it
is not a design problem, it is a missing idea.

**Don't steal:** the endless "I grew X to Y" framing if you don't have X and Y.
Fabricated specificity is the fastest way to lose a Lagos audience that knows
your actual work.

**Break it:** their format assumes a reader with patience. On WhatsApp status,
retention is one thumb-swipe — cut to five slides and skip the reward entirely,
ending on the ask.

## Justin Welsh — one idea, enormous whitespace

**What he does:** a single sentence per line, one idea per post, systems over
inspiration, and a clear "here's what I'd do" ending. Visually: almost nothing
on the page.

**Why it works:** low reading cost. The whitespace *is* the argument: this is
simple, therefore it might be true.

**Steal:** restraint. Our slides use 1 idea, 1 example, 1 line of body — the
rest is empty on purpose.

**Don't steal:** the "solopreneur" identity. Easywurld is a studio; the voice
should be a person, but the work should be shown.

**Break it:** Welsh rarely uses story. The personal carousels here (days 2, 5,
9) are longer and dirtier — a wet afternoon, an argument, a deletion. That is
deliberate: Lagos audiences respond to narrative more than to frameworks.

## Lara Acosta — the first line is the whole post

**What she does:** treats LinkedIn's two visible lines as the entire creative
brief. Personal credibility without a brag ("I got fired" not "I'm an expert").
Comment prompts that are genuinely useful.

**Steal:** write slide 1 and the caption's first line as *the same sentence*, so
the feed preview and the carousel reinforce each other. Day 1 does this.

**Don't steal:** the vulnerability-as-strategy tone if the story isn't real.
Readers can smell manufactured struggle.

**Break it:** her hooks are often about her. Ours are about the reader's loss —
"Your website is why they picked your competitor" — because a small-business
audience cares more about their Monday than your Tuesday.

## Jasmin Alić — the comment section as the second half of the post

**What he does:** ends with a prompt that gives people something specific to
say, then replies to everyone. Distribution comes from conversation, not reach.

**Steal:** day 10 is built entirely on this — "Drop your link, one sentence
back". Budget 20 minutes to reply, or the post is half-published.

**Don't steal:** the daily-question treadmill. One genuine invitation a month
beats thirty hollow "agree?" posts.

**Break it:** instead of asking for opinions, ask for *links* — the replies then
become free work you can quote (with permission) in next month's content.

## Chris Do — reframe the price, then show the contrast

**What he does:** moves the conversation from cost to value by changing the
unit of comparison. Teaches by placing two options side by side, visually.

**Steal:** the before/after slide (day 8). Two panels, same business, four
decisions — the reader sees the argument before reading it.

**Don't steal:** the premium-agency abstraction. "Value" means nothing to a
baker until it is ₦150,000 per wedding order.

**Break it:** Do's work assumes a client who can afford strategy. Our audience
often cannot — so the reframe must be *smaller*: one headline, one photo, one
button. Clarity before strategy.

## MrBeast — packaging before production

**What he does:** decides the title and thumbnail before filming, and abandons
ideas that cannot be packaged. Iterates on many concepts, not on one execution.

**Steal:** the three-hook test. Write three openers for the same body, post
them, keep the one that earns saves. The idea is the asset; the execution is
cheap.

**Don't steal:** scale thinking. He wins by spending; you win by being specific
to one street.

**Break it:** his retention logic says "cut everything slow". For a personal
story the slow part *is* the point — the two paragraphs where nothing happens
are what make the ending land.

---

## Sahil Bloom — name the framework

**What he does:** turns an observation into a named, numbered model the reader
can repeat. "The 3-2-1" is portable; a paragraph is not.

**Steal:** give your recurring ideas names you own — "the ten-second test",
"the quiet loss", "one idea per slide". Named things get repeated, and
repetition is reach.

**Don't steal:** framework inflation. Not every insight needs a diagram.

**Break it:** sometimes a framework is the coward's way to avoid saying
something uncomfortable. Day 5 has no framework — it has a client saying "this
is too simple" and not paying.

---

## Dan Koe — the contrarian reframe

**What he does:** "The problem isn't X. It's Y." Then defends Y with unusual
clarity, at length, with no fear of being wrong.

**Steal:** the myth-break (day 7: bigger vs clearer). It is the cheapest way to
earn attention honestly, because you are disagreeing with a belief the reader
already holds.

**Don't steal:** contrarianism as a brand. If you disagree with everything, the
disagreement stops meaning anything.

**Break it:** Koe writes long. For a carousel, deliver the reframe in one line
and let the reader finish the argument in their head.

---

## Gary Vaynerchuk — volume and empathy

**What he does:** enormous output, platform-native formats, and an unglamorous
focus on where attention already is.

**Steal:** publish the same idea in the native shape of each platform — a
LinkedIn document, an Instagram carousel, a WhatsApp status sequence — instead
of cross-posting one asset everywhere.

**Don't steal:** the volume as a substitute for quality. Ten sharp posts a month
beat a hundred hollow ones, especially when your name is on them.

## What this means for a Lagos audience specifically

Observations from the market, not from any one creator:

- **WhatsApp is the real distribution layer.** A status sequence that ends in
  "message CLEAR" reaches people who will never open LinkedIn.
- **Trust is visual and concrete.** A real photo of the work, a price, an
  address. Logos and adjectives read as noise.
- **Price honesty out-performs price mystery.** "From ₦45,000" filters and
  reassures at the same time.
- **Plainness is a status signal here.** Fluent, unadorned English reads as
  confident; borrowed agency-speak reads as junior.
- **Narrative beats framework.** A story about a client who didn't pay will
  travel further than a five-step model, in this market, this year.

## The exercise (do this before December)

1. **Three hooks, one body.** Post day 1 with three different openers across
   three weeks. Track **saves + shares**, not likes.
2. **Study ten accounts** in the niche — not the famous ones, the *local* ones
   with real engagement. Screenshot their best three posts. Ask of each: what
   was the first line, what was the cost, what was the reason to send it?
3. **Keep a swipe file** of hooks you wish you'd written. Steal structure, never
   sentences.
4. **Reply to every comment** for a month. The data you get from conversations
   is worth more than the data from analytics.

## The one rule above all rules

If a post follows this document perfectly and still feels like nothing, the
document is wrong for that post. The structure exists to make the sentence land
— when the sentence is strong enough, the structure can get out of the way.
