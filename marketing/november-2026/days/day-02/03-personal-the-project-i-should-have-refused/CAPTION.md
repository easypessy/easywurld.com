## LinkedIn

I should have refused a project. I took it because the money was good and I was new.

Fourteen services on one page. No photographs. No prices. A contact number belonging to a nephew who had already stopped answering.

The real problem was simple to name: nobody could tell what this business sold, so nobody bought. Instead of saying that, I designed around it. I made a beautiful page for a broken brief, and then spent three rounds of revisions defending work that was never the issue.

The client went quiet. I learned the most useful rule I have:

If I cannot name the problem in one sentence, I do not take the work.

That rule has cost me jobs. It has also meant that every client since got a result we could both point to — because we agreed on the problem before anyone opened a design file.

If you take work for a living: say the hard thing before you send the invoice. Clients can handle being told their brief is wrong on day one. They cannot handle discovering it in month three.

## Slide copy (for reference / edits)

1. **I should have said no to that project.**
2. **The money was good, and I was new.** — Fourteen services on one page. No photos. No prices. A phone number that belonged to a nephew who had stopped answering.
3. **I designed around the mess instead of naming it.** — I made a beautiful page for a business whose actual problem was that nobody could tell what they sold. I decorated the leak.
4. **Three revisions, then silence.** — The work was never wrong. The brief was. And I had accepted a brief I knew was broken because I wanted the job.
5. **If I can't name the problem, I don't take the work.** — That sentence has cost me jobs. It has also meant every client since then got a result we could both point at.
6. **Say the hard thing before you send the invoice.** — Not after. Before. The client can take it on day one; they cannot take it in month three. *Message CLEAR if you want a second opinion on a brief.*
